# CFP Survey - Free-Text Findings

Thematic analysis of the six open-ended questions. Built from the PII-scrubbed `freetext-extract.json` (10,862 comments across four languages), themed in-session. Themes coded with a multilingual keyword classifier verified against the corpus; comments may carry more than one theme. Counts are sound for headline figures; sensitive sub-claims should be spot-read before quoting.

Lens tags available per comment: gender, child age band, income (B40/M40 - see income note), disability, migration/refugee, low-wellbeing.

**Income note:** the survey's income brackets top out below the T20 threshold for most respondents, so the meaningful split is B40 vs M40, not a full three-way. T20 reads ~0% throughout. Confirm the income-question mapping before publishing income figures.

### Representative quotes (English; verbatim)

**Heat:** "Rising temperatures and more frequent heatwaves make outdoor play harder and can affect health and comfort." / "increasing heat, pollution and extreme weather"

**Overdevelopment / hills:** "The disappearing hills to make accommodations and development" / "a lot of high-rise apartments being built, less greenery, no proper recycling bin"

### Headline
The genuine environmental worries cluster tightly around **heat** (the single biggest, 19%), **air pollution / open burning**, and **loss of green space to overdevelopment** - the "disappearing hills" and high-rise construction thread is distinctive and locally specific to Penang. Heat and pollution concerns are evenly spread across income and wellbeing groups (a shared, universal anxiety - consistent with the eco-anxiety framing in the closed questions). Two notable lens signals: **overdevelopment concern skews M40** (more middle-income, possibly homeowner/quality-of-life driven), while the **social-environment and migrant threads skew strongly B40** - echoing 817, lower-income respondents more often read "environment" through a lens of neighbourhood safety and migration. For the situational analysis, this question most directly evidences **RO5 (climate & resilience)** and maps to AFC Outdoor Spaces and Health - with the clear message that heat and green-space loss are the lived environmental concerns, not abstract climate change.

### Note on data quality
This question had the messiest responses of the three analysed so far, because "environment" was interpreted two ways (natural vs social). The environmental themes (heat, pollution, green space, development, flooding) are robust; the social/foreigner themes are real but partly reflect question ambiguity. Worth a caveat in the report, and the environmental sub-set is the defensible core for RO5.

## Summary across all six questions

The free-text corpus (10,862 comments, four languages) converges on a clear, consistent story:

- **Safe, accessible play space and affordable activities** are the dominant asks (817, 824, 855) - sports first, then community, creative, and skills activities, with "free" recurring as the equity qualifier.
- **Safety** frames everything, but splits by respondent: parents emphasise **online/digital safety, enforcement and supervision** (833); children emphasise **physical road/traffic safety, crossings and lighting** (862). Both matter; they are complementary, not competing.
- **Heat and loss of green space** are the lived environmental worries (838) - concrete, not abstract climate anxiety - the strongest RO5 evidence.
- **A consistent equity signal:** lower-income (B40) and lower-wellbeing respondents voice greater safety concern and, in some questions, more anti-migrant sentiment. Low-wellbeing children specifically want **social connection** (855) and **visible protection** (862).
- **Emerging/forward-looking:** technology/AI/coding appears unprompted as a desired activity across 824 and 855 - parents and children both raise it.
- **Child-parent agreement** on activity priorities (824 vs 855) strengthens the programme-design case for MBPP/MBSP.

Method note: themes machine-coded via multilingual keyword classifiers verified against the corpus; substantive miss rates ~7-15% per question (higher where responses were short/vague or interpreted the question variously). Counts are sound for headline reporting; quotes are PII-scrubbed and should be vetted before public use.
